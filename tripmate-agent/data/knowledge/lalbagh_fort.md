# Lalbagh Fort (Qila Aurangabad)

Source: UNESCO World Heritage Centre Tentative List submission, “Mughal Forts on Fluvial Terrains in Dhaka.” This note paraphrases the State Party submission; inclusion on a Tentative List is not the same as inscription on the World Heritage List.

Lalbagh Fort, originally called Qila Aurangabad, is an incomplete 17th-century Mughal palace fort in Dhaka. Prince Muhammad Azam Shah, the third son of Emperor Aurangzeb, began construction in 1678 while serving as Subahdar of Bengal. The UNESCO Tentative List submission describes it as intended to serve as the Subahdar’s residence and administrative centre as well as a fortified defence. It represented Dhaka’s importance as the Mughal provincial capital.

After about 15 months, Aurangzeb recalled Prince Azam to suppress the Maratha rebellion. Azam’s successor, Shaista Khan, continued the work. Construction stopped after Shaista Khan’s daughter Iran Dukht, known as Pari Bibi, died in 1684. She had been engaged to Prince Azam, and her tomb is one of the fort’s central buildings. The account that Shaista Khan considered the site unlucky after her death is a commonly repeated story; the source documents the death and unfinished construction, not a supernatural curse.

The surviving complex includes the mosque, Pari Bibi’s tomb, and the Diwan-i-Aam (audience hall) with a hammam (bath). Excavations have also revealed remains of water supply and drainage systems, roof gardens, and fountains. Water channels connect the main buildings. These features show that the fort was planned as more than a military enclosure: it combined government, residence, defence, gardens, and water infrastructure, adapted to Dhaka’s riverine setting.

An interesting historical detail is that the Buriganga once flowed close to the fort, but the river later shifted away. The UNESCO submission notes that 19th-century paintings show the fort’s southern side beside the river. The fort’s original name, Qila Aurangabad, also changed: the name Lalbagh Fort came into use after preservation efforts began in 1844.

This note does not establish every popular legend about Pari Bibi or verify current visiting conditions.
